Finding 1 — the hallmark gap is universal, not occasional

Across 24 listings from 14 sellers, not one shows a photograph or description of a hallmark together with a maker's mark. Sellers publish stock numbers and style numbers; those are inventory references, not hallmarks. The FTC's guidance is explicit that a karat mark must be accompanied by the maker's name or trademark, and that without the trademark you should not buy. This is the strongest argument for treating every online gold purchase as unverified until an independent jeweler tests it.

Finding 2 — weight is withheld by exactly the sellers who could publish it

The three big retailers (Blue Nile, Brilliant Earth) publish no gram weight on any of the 11 rows checked, while smaller sellers (Allurez, Avianne, Jewelry Master, Solid Gold Jewelers, VeraByEden) do publish one. Without a weight you cannot run the price-vs-gold test before buying — you can only ask. That is a concrete, answerable question to put to the seller in writing, and a refusal is itself information.

Finding 3 — the one below-melt listing is a case study in why the test exists

The 7 g “14K solid” band in the case study was priced at $444.80. At the verified spot its gold content is 7.0 × 0.5833 = 4.083 g, worth $528.64. That is 0.84× melt — below the value of the metal alone. No jeweler sells solid gold below melt; the plausible explanations are that the gold is not what it claims, or the description is wrong. The listing is now unavailable, which removes the chance to test that hypothesis — another reason to keep documentation.

Finding 4 — “setting only” quietly breaks price comparisons

14 of 24 rows are setting prices with the center diamond excluded. A $870 setting plus a 1 ct diamond is a very different purchase from a $969 complete ring. Any comparison that ignores the basis is wrong. The table therefore carries a Price basis label on every row and a flag (F10) explaining it.

Finding 5 — return windows are shorter than buyers assume

Blue Nile's 30 days excludes resized and engraved items. On Etsy the windows we read were 7, 14, 21 and 30 days depending on the shop. If your plan is “buy, inspect with a jeweler, return if wrong”, the window must outlast the shipping time plus the jeweler's appointment. VeraByEden's 7 days is tight for an international shipment; RAKSHAKJEWELS ships from India with a 14-day window.

What would change the conclusion

  • A listing publishing weight + an XRF report + a hallmark photo with maker's mark would move that row from “verify on arrival” to “pre-verified”. None exists in this dataset.
  • A seller publishing a written return policy that survives resizing would remove the biggest practical risk in the checklist.
  • Independent reputation data for the Etsy shops (beyond Etsy's own ratings) would let us rank them. None was verifiable from an independent source, so none is claimed.

Bottom line

How to use this list

Use it as a shortlist of real, currently-listed rings with traceable prices — not as a guarantee. Pick rows where the seller answers the weight and hallmark questions in writing, pay by credit card, keep the 30-day window intact, and have the ring weighed and XRF-tested before you wear it. If a row has no weight and the seller won't provide one, walk away.
